Internship · Carilion Clinic Biodesign

Clinical Biodesign Internship

This was my first chance to study engineering problems inside a hospital. I researched needs reported by clinicians, tested a liquid-measurement mechanism, and saw how much patient safety and clinical workflow shape even a small design decision.

RoleBiomedical Engineering InternMentorBriana ApgarLocationCarilion Clinic · Roanoke, Virginia

Where I started

Before I could design anything, I had to understand the problem.

There was no neat assignment waiting for me with one correct answer. I worked through a database of needs that clinicians had noticed in operating rooms, patient rooms, pharmacies, and other parts of the hospital.

At first, several of the problems sounded straightforward. Once I began asking when the issue happened, who it affected, and what staff were already doing to work around it, each one became more complicated. That research changed the way I approach design. I now spend more time defining the problem before deciding what the solution should be.

Clinical needs database

The problems I kept coming back to

I did not turn all four into finished designs. I researched them, compared their impact and feasibility, and used that work to choose a realistic direction.

01

IV tubing

Tubing can kink when it is placed, moved, or caught at the wrong angle. I started looking beyond the tube itself and asking how quickly a nurse could see the problem and what was happening around the patient when it occurred.

02

Syringe measurement

Small measurement errors can matter. I looked at the mechanism, printed markings, resistance, and the way someone holds the device to understand where inconsistency might come from.

03

Catheters and drains

Flow and positioning change as a patient moves. That made these needs more complicated than they first appeared, because the device has to work with both the body and the care routine around it.

04

Surgical workflow

I also looked at problems inside procedures: where instruments are placed, how many steps a task takes, and what makes a measurement harder to complete safely under pressure.

Main design study

A closer look at liquid measurement

I focused on a syringe and micropipette-related problem because accuracy connects directly to patient care. I first took time to understand the piston, volume dial, barrel, calibration, and the small internal features that control movement.

I broke the mechanism into smaller parts using diagrams, research, hands-on testing, and early CAD ideas. I considered ridges, barrel structure, tolerances, friction, and how a user sets and checks a measurement. A few of my first ideas created more problems than they solved, so I backed up, changed the assumption, and tried again.

  1. Understand the mechanism

    I traced how a movement of the hand changes the internal position and liquid volume.

  2. Find the sources of error

    I looked at calibration, friction, tolerances, readability, and repeated use.

  3. Think about the person using it

    I considered how quickly and confidently someone could set and verify a dose.

  4. Revise instead of forcing it

    When an idea introduced a new problem, I used that failure to guide the next version.

Hands-on testing

The mechanism made more sense once it was in my hand.

Feeling the resistance and watching the plunger move gave me information that a diagram could not. Repeating the same action also made it easier to notice where control or consistency could break down.

The test reminded me that accuracy is not only about the number on the device. Grip, feedback, visibility, and the ease of repeating a motion all matter to the person using it.

Clinical observation

Surgery made every design constraint feel real.

I scrubbed in and observed a robot-assisted esophagectomy. I watched the team communicate, saw how sterile technique controlled movement, and paid attention to where the da Vinci system and other devices fit into the procedure.

I also observed post-operative care, learned about aspiration risk and recovery, saw how surgical research and IRB approval work, and toured a specimens lab. By the end, I understood that a device is not successful just because it works on a desk. It has to work around patients, clinicians, time pressure, and strict safety requirements.

What stayed with me

I left with a different way of looking at problems.

I became more patient with research and more comfortable admitting when the evidence did not support my first idea.

Context changes everything

I now ask what happens to the patient, the care team, the room, and the steps before and after a device is used.

Safety is part of the design

Usability, contamination risk, measurement error, and recovery cannot be added at the end.

A failed idea still teaches you

If I can explain why something failed, I have useful information for the next version.
